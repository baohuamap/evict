\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{The confidence signal bottleneck.}
The central empirical finding of this paper is that conformal calibration for LLM-based alert triage is mechanically correct but limited by the quality of the self-consistency confidence signal. The vote-share unanimity rate---the fraction of $k=5$ samples that agree unanimously---varies from 36\% (reasoning models) to 98\% (code-specialized MoE), and this variation maps directly to ECE (0.335 vs.\ 0.620) and calibration benefit ($+1.1$pp vs.\ $<$1pp). This finding has two implications. First, it identifies the bottleneck: improving calibration in the EVICT pipeline requires improving the confidence signal, not the calibration algorithm. Second, it reveals that model architecture and training objective---not model size---are the primary determinants of confidence quality. A 14B reasoning model outperforms all 32B+ standard models on calibration, contradicting the conventional scaling narrative.

\paragraph{Two paths to good calibration.}
Our results identify two distinct mechanisms by which models achieve low ECE. \emph{Reasoning models} (R1-Distill-14B) achieve ECE 0.335 through diverse confidence: the chain-of-thought process causes genuine disagreement across $k=5$ samples when the model is uncertain, producing informative confidence scores. \emph{Frontier-lite models} (Claude Haiku 4.5) achieve ECE 0.373 through high base precision: when confident predictions are usually correct, ECE is naturally lower even with degenerate vote-share (90\% unanimous). The ideal model for EVICT combines both paths---a frontier reasoning model with both high accuracy and diverse confidence---which our projections (Table~\ref{tab:frontier_proj}) suggest will achieve ECE 0.20--0.30 with $+3$--$10$pp calibration benefit.

\paragraph{The code-specialization paradox.}
Code-specialized models (DS-Coder-V2-Lite, Qwen-Coder-32B) exhibit the most degenerate confidence (86--98\% unanimous) yet achieve the best FP rejection on real-world alerts (DS-Coder-V2-Lite: TN=184, the highest of all models). This paradox---code specialization improves semantic understanding of code safety while degrading uncertainty quantification---suggests that code-trained models recognize safe patterns more effectively but are overconfident in their judgments. A practical implication is that code-specialized models may be better suited as the base classifier in a two-stage system (high-precision FP rejection), with a reasoning model providing the calibration signal.

\paragraph{Limitations and scope.}
The primary limitation is empirical scope. The conformal PoC uses three CWE classes on a synthetic benchmark (Juliet); the CWE-Bench-Java validation uses 45 of 201 projects (156 Docker images unavailable). The symbolic escalator's Z3 backend returns UNKNOWN for Java string-based path constraints, limiting escalation to sanitization detection only; JPF integration remains future work. The frontier model projections (Table~\ref{tab:frontier_proj}) are estimates derived from scaling relationships, not direct measurements, and carry $\pm$5pp uncertainty. The evidence-conditioned prompt shows no precision improvement over evidence-free prompting in the PoC, suggesting that the EvidencePack extraction may need richer content (full data-flow, better slicing) or prompt restructuring. Conformal validity holds under exchangeability between calibration and test data, an assumption weakened by cross-project shift.

\paragraph{Future directions.}
The findings motivate three concrete directions: (1) \textbf{richer confidence signals}---token-level log-probabilities, semantic consistency, or cross-model disagreement may provide more discriminative signals than vote-share for non-reasoning models; (2) \textbf{frontier reasoning model evaluation}---direct measurement of Claude Opus 4, GPT-4.5, and other frontier reasoning models in the EVICT pipeline; and (3) \textbf{JPF-based symbolic escalation}---replacing the Z3 integer-proxy with Java PathFinder for real path-feasibility checking on deferred alerts.
