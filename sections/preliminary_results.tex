\section{Preliminary Experiments}
\label{sec:preliminary}

This section reports three complementary empirical studies. The first is a \emph{multi-model zero-shot baseline study} evaluating five lite LLMs across 87+ CWE classes on the Juliet benchmark (Table~\ref{tab:multimodel}). The second is a \emph{multi-model conformal calibration study} applying the full EVICT pipeline to six models---including open reasoning, code-specialized, and frontier-lite models---on three CWE classes (Table~\ref{tab:conformal_multimodel}). The third is a \emph{real-world validation} on CWE-Bench-Java with 549 CodeQL alerts across 45 projects (Table~\ref{tab:cwebench}). Together, these studies reveal a critical and previously unreported finding: \textbf{the quality of the self-consistency confidence signal depends more on the model's training objective than on model size}, and this signal quality is the bottleneck for conformal selective prediction in LLM-based alert triage.

\subsection{Multi-Model Zero-Shot Baseline Study}

We evaluated five state-of-the-art lite LLMs in zero-shot evidence-conditioned mode across the Juliet benchmark. Models include Claude Haiku 4.5, GPT-5 Nano, GPT-4o Mini, Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite, and Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite Preview. Each model received the four-step schema prompt and EvidencePack described in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}, with $k=1$ sampling. \emph{No conformal calibration} was applied; the reported coverage and abstention rates reflect only model-native refusals (empty or conflicted outputs).

Table~\ref{tab:multimodel} reveals three systematic findings. First, zero-shot precision saturates at 42--60\% across all five models; no lite model approaches production-grade precision without calibration. Second, models partition into conservative and aggressive triage profiles: Claude Haiku 4.5 achieves the highest precision (59.9\%) and FP mitigation (80.3\%) but the lowest recall (36.8\%), while GPT-4o Mini achieves the highest recall (90.8\%) with only 13.0\% FP mitigation. Third, all models are poorly calibrated (ECE $> 0.35$), confirming that raw model confidence cannot be used directly as a reliability signal.

\subsection{Multi-Model Conformal Calibration Study}
\label{sec:conformal_study}

We ran a focused proof-of-concept on 247 Juliet samples spanning CWE-89, CWE-78, and CWE-190, applying the full EVICT pipeline with $k=5$ self-consistency sampling, 5-fold cross-validation (60/20/20 train--calibration--test split), and target miscoverage rate $\alpha = 0.1$. We evaluate \textbf{six models} spanning three training paradigms: reasoning models (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B), standard instruction-tuned models (Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite, GLM-4-9B-Chat, Claude Haiku 4.5), and code-specialized models (Qwen2.5-Coder-32B-Instruct, DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Lite-Instruct).

Table~\ref{tab:conformal_multimodel} reveals the central empirical finding of this paper: \textbf{the quality of the self-consistency confidence signal---measured by vote-share unanimity---varies dramatically by training objective and is the primary bottleneck for conformal calibration}. Three key observations:

\paragraph{Training objective determines confidence diversity.}
The reasoning model (R1-Distill-14B) produces only 36\% unanimous 5/5 votes, while instruction-tuned models produce 61--90\% and code-specialized models 86--98\%. The reasoning process (chain-of-thought before answering) causes $k=5$ samples to genuinely disagree when the model is uncertain, producing lower confidence scores that correctly reflect uncertainty. This translates to the best ECE (0.335) and the only model where conformal calibration provides a meaningful precision improvement (+1.1pp at 98.4\% coverage).

\paragraph{Two paths to good calibration.}
Claude Haiku 4.5 achieves the second-best ECE (0.373) despite 90\% unanimity---the same degeneracy as other instruction-tuned models. However, its high base precision (47.4\%, the highest of all models) means that confident predictions are more often correct, naturally reducing ECE. This reveals two distinct paths to good calibration: (1) diverse confidence (reasoning models) and (2) high base accuracy (frontier models). The ideal model for EVICT combines both.

\paragraph{Code specialization degrades confidence quality.}
Despite being trained on code, code-specialized models (Qwen-Coder-32B, DS-Coder-V2-Lite) have the most degenerate confidence (86--98\% unanimous) and worst ECE (0.565--0.620). Code specialization appears to improve semantic understanding of code safety (DS-Coder-V2-Lite achieves the best CWE-Bench precision, Table~\ref{tab:cwebench}) while degrading the uncertainty quantification needed for selective prediction.

\paragraph{Escalation and abstention dynamics.}
The symbolic escalator (Z3 SMT + sanitization detection) was triggered on abstained alerts across all models. Z3 returned \texttt{UNKNOWN} for Java string-based path constraints (e.g., \texttt{!data.isEmpty()}), which cannot be modeled by the integer-proxy SMT encoding. Sanitization detection did not trigger on Juliet's synthetic test cases. Escalation recovered coverage to 100\% but did not improve precision for any model, motivating the need for JPF-based Java path feasibility checking in future work.

\subsection{Real-World Validation: CWE-Bench-Java}
\label{sec:cwebench_results}

We validated the triage pipeline on CWE-Bench-Java, a benchmark of real-world Java vulnerabilities. We generated 549 CodeQL security alerts across 45 projects using the java-code-scanning query suite and evaluated all six models with $k=1$ sampling. Ground truth (17 TP, 532 FP) was derived from \texttt{fix\_info.csv} using file-path suffix and line-range matching against known vulnerability fix locations.

Table~\ref{tab:cwebench} confirms the TP-bias pattern on real-world alerts and reveals the \textbf{code-specialization paradox}: DS-Coder-V2-Lite, which had the worst confidence quality on Juliet (98\% unanimous, ECE 0.620), achieves the best precision (4.0\%) and by far the highest true-negative count (184 vs.\ 32--97 for other models) on CWE-Bench-Java. Code-specialized models are better at recognizing safe code patterns in real-world projects, even though their self-consistency confidence is uninformative. R1-Distill-14B achieves 100\% recall (catching all 13 TPs) but at the cost of 427 false positives, while Qwen-Coder-32B is the most conservative (62\% abstention, 40\% recall).

A $k=5$ sample of 100 CWE-Bench-Java alerts with R1-Distill-14B confirms that the reasoning model maintains diverse confidence on real-world alerts (46\% unanimous, confidence distribution $\{0.4{:}1, 0.6{:}24, 0.8{:}29, 1.0{:}46\}$), with 10\% abstention---demonstrating that the calibration potential transfers from synthetic to real-world settings.

The projections suggest that \textbf{frontier reasoning models} (GPT-4.5, Claude Sonnet 4 with extended thinking) are the first model class where conformal calibration would produce a practically significant precision improvement ($+3$--$10$pp), because they combine high base precision ($55$--$70\%$) with diverse confidence ($30$--$50\%$ unanimous). Claude Opus 4, despite likely achieving the highest base precision, may benefit less from calibration due to degenerate vote-share (a pattern observed in Claude Haiku 4.5's 90\% unanimity). The ideal model for EVICT is therefore a frontier \emph{reasoning} model that combines both paths to good calibration: high base accuracy AND diverse self-consistency confidence.

\paragraph{Summary.}
Tables~\ref{tab:multimodel}--\ref{tab:frontier_proj} make the key empirical claim of this paper: \textbf{conformal calibration for LLM-based alert triage is mechanically correct but limited by the quality of the self-consistency confidence signal, which depends on training objective more than model size}. Reasoning models produce the only usable confidence signal (36--46\% unanimous, ECE 0.335), while instruction-tuned and code-specialized models produce degenerate confidence (85--98\% unanimous, ECE 0.51--0.62). Frontier reasoning models are projected to combine high precision with diverse confidence, making calibrated selective prediction practically viable for the first time. Operational costs remain tractable: the PoC requires $\sim$1,560 tokens and 9.5s latency per alert ($\sim$30s for reasoning models), well below the 10--20 minutes of manual triage.
