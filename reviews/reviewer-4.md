# Review 4 — Systems / Applied ML Perspective

**Rating:** 4 — Weak reject
**Confidence:** 3 — Somewhat confident

---

## Summary

EVICT is a four-stage pipeline for LLM-assisted static-analysis alert triage: SARIF-based evidence extraction, schema-guided LLM classification, conformal calibration with abstention, and conditional symbolic verification. The paper's thesis is that framing triage as selective prediction — with explicit abstention and calibrated uncertainty — produces safer automation than forced classification. The empirical basis is a 1,000-sample Juliet study; a larger evaluation is planned but not yet executed.

---

## Strengths

1. **Clear system design.** The four-stage pipeline is well-specified, with distinct responsibilities at each stage and clean algorithmic descriptions in the appendix.

2. **Decision-theoretic coherence.** The cost-sensitive objective, asymmetric cost parameters, and abstention as a first-class action form a consistent decision-theoretic story. This is meaningfully better than systems that treat confidence thresholds as opaque tuning parameters.

3. **Honest about incompleteness.** The paper repeatedly and explicitly flags that the current evidence is insufficient for the deployment claims, that several baseline rows are simulated, and that a real submission requires the full evaluation. This is rare and commendable.

4. **Detailed evaluation plan.** The proposed multi-dataset, multi-analyzer, multi-ablation study in Section 6 is thorough and would, if executed, produce the evidence needed to support the paper's claims.

---

## Weaknesses

### W1 — The paper cannot be evaluated as a completed research contribution

The central issue is structural: this paper describes a planned study with partial preliminary results, not a completed one. The abstract and introduction present EVICT's contributions in the present tense ("achieves 91.2% precision," "adds 2.8 precision points") without adequately flagging that:
- Baselines LLM4FPM, LLM4PFA, AdaTaint, and Agentic LLM are fabricated ("simulated placeholders" per the paper's own Table 1 caption).
- All results come from a single synthetic corpus with a single analyzer.
- The paper itself states: "A real submission would still require genuine cross-project results, multi-analyzer evidence, and measured baseline implementations."

NeurIPS reviews a submitted paper, not a projected one. A paper that describes its own results as insufficient for a "real submission" has answered its own evaluation question.

### W2 — The simulated baselines are not an acceptable substitute for empirical comparison

Tables 1 and 3 contain rows explicitly labeled "(sim.)" representing LLM4FPM, LLM4PFA, AdaTaint, and Agentic LLM Workflow. These numbers were generated by the authors, not from running those systems. Including fabricated comparison data in a results table — regardless of labeling — creates a misleading impression of the paper's empirical standing and makes it impossible for reviewers to assess whether EVICT actually improves on the state of the art. The description "intended to approximate the shape of a full comparative section" is not a substitute for an actual comparative section.

This is the single most significant issue in the paper. If resubmitted, all simulated rows must be removed or replaced with real measurements.

### W3 — The 12.5% symbolic correction rate (23/184) does not support the precision claim

Table 2 reports that symbolic checks corrected 23 of 184 escalated alerts (12.5%) and delivered +2.8 pp precision. However:
- The direction of corrections (FP→TP vs. TP→FP) is not reported.
- Whether the 15 UNKNOWN SMT outcomes were treated as abstentions, forced as FPs, or handed to bounded symbolic execution is not specified.
- The claim that symbolic verification "adds 2.8 precision points" is stated in the abstract as if it were a robust finding, when it rests on 23 instances in a synthetic study.

**Suggested fix:** Report correction direction. Clarify UNKNOWN handling. Qualify the 2.8 pp figure as a preliminary estimate from a small controlled study.

### W4 — Coverage metric conflation

The paper defines coverage as $\mathbb{P}[g(x)=1]$, i.e., the fraction of alerts on which the system makes a prediction. But EVICT reports 87.3% coverage after the calibrated stage with 12.7% abstention — yet precision is also 91.2% on the accepted set. It is not stated clearly whether:
- Abstained alerts that are later resolved by symbolic verification count toward coverage.
- "Coverage" in the abstract refers to the pre-escalation or post-escalation acceptance rate.
- The 91.2% precision is pre-escalation (conformal set) or post-escalation (including symbolic corrections).

These distinctions matter because the conformal guarantee covers only the pre-escalation predictor. If the reported 91.2% is a post-escalation figure, it is not guaranteed by the theoretical analysis.

### W5 — Deployment practicality of the symbolic back-end is understated

The paper targets "more than 50,000 alerts" in the full evaluation. At 18.4% escalation and 4.2s average symbolic verification, this implies roughly 3,900 escalated alerts × 4.2s ≈ 4.5 hours of solver time on a dataset of that size — assuming perfect parallelism. In practice, solver invocations contend for memory, the 8.1% UNKNOWN rate grows on more complex real-world code, and KLEE/SPF do not scale gracefully to large Java or C codebases. The paper mentions "short timeouts" and "conservative treatment" of UNKNOWN outcomes but does not provide any concrete solver-resource profiling beyond the 4.2s average. For a system-oriented contribution, this gap is significant.

---

## Questions for Authors

1. In the abstract and Section 1, results are stated without qualification (e.g., "EVICT achieves 91.2% precision"). Table 1 footnotes clarify that comparison rows are simulated. How do you intend to reframe the abstract-level claims to accurately reflect the scope of the completed study?

2. Is the 91.2% precision a pre-escalation or post-escalation figure? Does it include the 23 symbolic corrections?

3. For the UNKNOWN SMT outcomes: what decision does EVICT emit for an alert that (a) was abstained by conformal calibration and (b) receives UNKNOWN from Z3 and then an exhausted-but-inconclusive result from SPF/KLEE?

4. What is the total solver-time budget for the full 50,000-alert evaluation? Do you have benchmarks showing that the 4.2s average from Juliet is representative of real-world code complexity in NASCAR and DZA?

5. On DZA, where labels derive from bug-fix deltas, what fraction of "fixed" alerts in the commit history were actually true positives vs. incidental suppressions? Have you validated a sample manually?

---

## Minor Issues

- Table 1 caption: "Rows marked '(sim.)' are simulated baselines included for this mock submission." The phrase "mock submission" should not appear in a submission to peer review.
- Section 5 last paragraph: "In this simulated manuscript…" — same concern.
- The appendix prompt template is too brief. A full worked example for one CWE would materially improve reproducibility.

---

## Overall Assessment

The EVICT design is thoughtful and the problem framing is genuinely valuable. I do not question the authors' ability to execute the planned evaluation. My concern is that the current submission presents an incomplete study — with fabricated comparison data in the results tables, a single synthetic dataset, and self-acknowledged insufficiency for a real submission — as if it were a completed NeurIPS paper. The gap between the paper's claims and its evidence is too large to bridge with author responses. I recommend rejection and encourage resubmission once the full evaluation is complete.

**Score: 4 — Weak reject**
