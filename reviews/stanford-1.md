Summary
This paper introduces EVICT, a framework for triaging static analysis alerts by explicitly formulating the task as selective prediction with calibrated abstention. The approach combines schema-guided, evidence-conditioned LLM reasoning, conformal calibration to convert raw scores into operationally meaningful confidence, and conditional symbolic verification (SMT and bounded symbolic execution) on uncertain or high-severity cases. The paper derives risk–coverage generalization bounds and finite-sample conformal guarantees, and reports preliminary results on 1,000 Juliet samples, showing improved precision and calibration over evidence-free prompting while maintaining high coverage.

Strengths
Technical novelty and innovation
Frames alert triage as a selective prediction problem with explicit cost-sensitive risk, abstention, and coverage, moving beyond forced classification commonly used in LLM-based triage.
Integrates conformal calibration to yield distribution-free finite-sample guarantees for prediction sets, providing an interpretable mechanism for abstention and thresholding.
Proposes a neuro-symbolic escalation policy that triggers SMT-based checks and symbolic execution only when calibrated confidence is low or the stakes are high, balancing scalability with rigor.
Employs schema-guided prompting tied to analyzer evidence (slice, taint flow, path constraints), which promotes inspectable reasoning and aligns with the claimed calibration benefits.
Experimental rigor and validation
Preliminary study separates contributions: evidence vs calibration vs symbolic escalation, and reports improvements in precision and ECE alongside risk–coverage behavior.
Includes reliability diagrams and selective risk–coverage curves that are appropriate diagnostics for the selective prediction framing.
Outlines a substantial, multi-dataset evaluation plan (Juliet, NASCAR, DZA, CWE-Bench-Java) across multiple analyzers, with clear success criteria and ablations.
Clarity of presentation
Clear problem formulation (cost-sensitive selective risk) and end-to-end system overview with stages that map cleanly to the proposed methodology.
Theoretical results are standard but well-motivated here, and the practical interpretation (coverage–risk and abstention cost) is explained in deployment terms.
Appendices provide algorithms, prompt schemas, and calibration procedures, aiding reproducibility.
Significance of contributions
Addresses a pressing and high-impact problem: the prevalence of false positives in static analysis and the need for trustworthy, auditable triage rather than purely automated suppression.
The selective-prediction perspective and calibrated abstention are well-aligned with operational needs for safe automation in software security.
The hybrid neuro-symbolic approach is compelling for improving trust and reducing hallucination risks relative to pure LLM pipelines.
Weaknesses
Technical limitations or concerns
Conformal guarantees rely on exchangeability; the paper acknowledges project shift but does not yet demonstrate how calibration holds up across projects or analyzers in practice (e.g., group-conditional or project-aware conformal methods, or coverage auditing under shift).
The construction of conformity scores from LLM outputs (logits, entropy, self-consistency) is underspecified; the choice of nonconformity function and its impact on singleton-set frequency and abstention rate are not rigorously analyzed.
The risk bound uses VC-dimension-style arguments that may not be informative for large LLMs and complex rejectors; a discussion of effective complexity or algorithm-dependent bounds would strengthen the theory’s practical relevance.
The interaction between conformal calibration and conditional symbolic escalation is nontrivial; after escalation alters decisions on a subset, the validity and semantics of the calibrated confidence are unclear unless carefully separated or recalibrated post-escalation.
Experimental gaps or methodological issues
Current empirical evidence is limited to 1,000 Juliet samples and uses a single analyzer; no cross-project, real-world, or multi-analyzer results are shown, despite being central to the paper’s claims about deployment viability.
Missing comparative baselines to contemporary neuro-symbolic/LLM triage systems (e.g., AdaTaint, LLM4PFA, LLM4FPM) and to agentic LLM workflows; the absence of strong baselines makes it hard to assess incremental benefit beyond evidence conditioning and calibration.
Limited reporting of costs (LLM tokens, latency, solver timeouts) and a lack of explicit cost–coverage tradeoff curves, particularly crucial for practical deployment and to justify the selective escalation policy.
No analysis of false-negative risks under abstention thresholds (e.g., how often true vulnerabilities are deferred or dismissed), or sensitivity to asymmetric costs (α, β, γ), which are central to the paper’s cost-sensitive framing.
Clarity or presentation issues
Definitions around the selective risk metric Rsel and its computation with abstention could be clearer; it is not always explicit whether ECE and F1 are reported on the accepted set or entire set.
Figures contain minor artifacts and duplications (likely from PDF extraction), and some algorithmic hyperparameters (timeouts, calibration α, selection of singleton-set acceptance) are not specified concretely in the main text.
The paper mentions both temperature scaling and conformal prediction; the interaction, if any, between these calibration methods is not clearly articulated.
Missing related work or comparisons
While AdaTaint and several recent LLM-triage papers are cited, there is no experimental comparison; given AdaTaint’s similar neuro-symbolic validation aims, a head-to-head is important.
Recent studies on agentic LLM frameworks for SAST FP filtering and earlier ML-based FP filtering on Juliet-derived corpora would enrich the empirical context and highlight differences (e.g., formal abstention vs heuristic confidence).
Detailed Comments
Technical soundness evaluation
The selective prediction framing with a rejector, and the use of conformal prediction to obtain finite-sample guarantees, are appropriate and conceptually sound for high-stakes triage. The cost-sensitive abstention rule is a standard and well-justified decision-theoretic choice.
However, the claim of distribution-free guarantees should be carefully scoped: the conformal validity holds for the set-valued predictor under exchangeability. Once the pipeline introduces conditional symbolic escalation that can flip predictions or prune uncertain cases, the overall system’s coverage/validity properties may depart from the calibrated stage unless the escalation is treated as a post-prediction audit that does not change the selective acceptance mechanism, or unless calibration is re-run on the post-escalation outputs. Clarifying this sequencing and its implications for guarantees would strengthen the technical story.
The theoretical bound (Theorem 1) is standard but may be of limited practical bite here. A brief discussion about the induced hypothesis/rejector class, potential data-dependent complexity measures, or empirical calibration-of-confidence-based thresholds would bridge theory to practice more convincingly.
Experimental evaluation assessment
The preliminary Juliet results are promising: +7.7 pp precision over an evidence-free baseline at similar coverage, better ECE, and a plausible cost profile for symbolic checks (18.4% triggered, ~4.2s average). The reliability diagrams and risk–coverage plots are the right diagnostics for this framing.
That said, the evidence base is too small for a NeurIPS-level empirical contribution. The paper’s central claims—robust calibrated abstention under shift, viability across analyzers/datasets, and the practical value of conditional symbolic escalation—need multi-dataset, cross-project, and multi-tool experiments, plus cost/latency analysis. Sensitivity analyses over α, γ, and coverage targets would make the cost-sensitive framing actionable. A user study (even a small one) demonstrating reduced triage time and improved trust would significantly strengthen the paper.
Comparison with related work
AdaTaint also follows a neuro-symbolic paradigm, selectively validating flows with constraint checks and symbolic reasoning. EVICT’s main differentiator is the explicit selective-prediction formulation with calibrated abstention and finite-sample semantics via conformal prediction. An empirical comparison would make this distinction concrete.
LLM4PFA emphasizes path feasibility reasoning, but (according to the paper) still performs forced classification. Showing that EVICT achieves better selective risk at comparable or higher coverage—and demonstrating calibration robustness under project shift—would be compelling.
Recent work on agentic LLM triage shows substantial FP reductions by orchestrating tool use and multi-step reasoning. EVICT’s calibrated abstention and symbolic escalation could be seen as a principled alternative; comparing against a strong agentic baseline would help position EVICT among practical deployment options.
Earlier Juliet-based FP filtering using classical ML demonstrates strong precision/recall on synthetic corpora. EVICT should show gains that persist on natural code, given its emphasis on calibrated abstention and evidence conditioning.
Broader impact and significance
The paper’s central message—that safe automation in static analysis should center on calibrated abstention and explicit deferral—is timely and important for real-world adoption. The human-in-the-loop posture, with inspectable rationales and auditable symbolic certificates, addresses over-trust risks common in LLM-based tools.
Risks remain: potential over-reliance on incomplete evidence, privacy concerns with API models, and the danger of suppressing true vulnerabilities if abstention thresholds are mis-specified or calibration drifts under shift. The proposed conservative policies and feedback loops are reasonable mitigations; empirical validation of these mitigations would be valuable.
Questions for Authors
How exactly are conformity scores constructed from LLM outputs for binary TP/FP? Please specify the nonconformity function(s) used (e.g., margin, entropy, self-consistency), how you normalize across label options, and how this choice affects the rate of singleton sets and abstentions.
What is the sequencing between conformal calibration and symbolic escalation? If symbolic checks can overturn the LLM decision on a subset, does the conformal validity still apply to the overall system output, or is calibration validity only claimed for the pre-escalation stage? Would a post-escalation recalibration be feasible?
How are α, β, and γ chosen in practice, and how sensitive are results to these cost parameters? Could you include a sensitivity study showing precision/recall/coverage/abstention as a function of γ (and perhaps α/β ratios) to guide deployment?
Under cross-project or cross-analyzer shift, what calibration strategy do you use (e.g., project-conditional conformal, Mondrian CP, recalibration windows)? Can you report coverage and validity diagnostics that quantify deviation from nominal under shift?
Can you provide detailed runtime and cost metrics: tokens/alert for the LLM, wall-clock per alert, and solver resource usage? How do these scale with alert complexity, and how do they compare with agentic LLM baselines?
How is ground truth established in the Juliet experiments (e.g., mapping alerts to manifest labels), and how do you guard against leakage between training/calibration/test splits?
Could you compare EVICT head-to-head with AdaTaint, LLM4PFA, and one strong agentic LLM pipeline on at least one real-world dataset (e.g., NASCAR), reporting precision, coverage, ECE, and cost?
How do you treat abstentions in ECE and F1 computation—are metrics reported on the accepted set only? If so, can you also report end-to-end selective risk and coverage-adjusted metrics to make tradeoffs explicit?
When evidence is incomplete (e.g., due to reflection or native calls), do you explicitly model this as a feature for the rejector? How often does incompleteness lead to abstention versus incorrect FP/TP decisions?
Could you detail the exact prompt schema, hyperparameters (timeouts, solver bounds), and model access modes used (logits vs API, self-consistency protocol) to facilitate replication?
Overall Assessment
EVICT presents a well-motivated and conceptually coherent framework that reorients static analysis triage around calibrated selective prediction and targeted symbolic verification. The combination of evidence-conditioned prompting, conformal calibration, and conditional symbolic checks is novel in this precise formulation and has strong practical appeal for safe deployment. The theoretical framing is appropriate and the preliminary results suggest real promise, especially in calibration and risk–coverage control. However, the current empirical validation is too limited for a top-tier venue: it lacks multi-dataset, cross-project, and multi-analyzer results; strong baselines; and detailed cost analyses that are central to the deployment claim. Clarifying how conformal guarantees interact with symbolic escalation and providing robust evaluations under shift would significantly elevate the paper. I view this as a promising direction that could be above the acceptance bar with a strengthened experimental section and tighter integration of the guarantees with the full pipeline. At present, I lean toward a weak reject due to insufficient empirical substantiation, while recognizing the paper’s clear potential and contribution to a more principled view of LLM-assisted static analysis triage.
